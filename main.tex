\begin{abstract}
Efforts to integrate multiple hazards, exposure and vulnerabilities into coherent multi-risk frameworks have advanced over the past three decades, yet the field continues to lack operationalisable metrics to assess whether increasing modelling complexity has improved predictive or decision-making efficiency. This paper argues that the bias–variance tradeoff, originally from statistical learning theory, can serve as an analytical lens to interpret this trajectory and to ask whether the field's growing complexity reflects genuine progress or an "illusion of optimality". Without explicit measures of informational efficiency, risk integration could become performative rather than functional. We acknowledge that the path toward optimality must be reconciled with the actionability required by practitioners, and that once social components become central to an assessment, what counts as "optimal" is no longer technically determined but becomes relational, context-dependent, and contested. The paper therefore does not prescribe a universal optimum. Instead, it defines informational efficiency as the marginal gain in decision-relevant output obtained for each added increment of model complexity, and proposes a set of quantitative and qualitative markers---spanning statistical, decisional, temporal, institutional, interpretive, and social dimensions---through which this gain can be assessed.

\end{abstract}

 \section{Introduction}
Multi-risk assessments are inherently complex, requiring the integration of compounding hazards, dynamic exposure, and evolving societal vulnerabilities. Over the past three decades, tools and methods have advanced significantly to address these multiple layers of complexity. The bias--variance framework \citep{geman1992neural} provides a pragmatic analytical argument for a long-standing challenge in risk science: determining when integration enhances understanding and when it merely amplifies uncertainty. Rooted in statistics and popularised in machine learning, the concept describes the tradeoff between underfitting (high bias) and overfitting (high variance) \citep{geman1992neural, hastie2009elements}. Lower predictive error implies reduced uncertainty over outcomes, which in turn affects the expected decision \citep{domingos2000unified}. \citet{Dunant2021} first applied this framework to multi-hazard risk assessment, arguing that single-hazard approaches are biased and that multi-hazard integration is needed. This paper addresses the complementary question: once integration is under way, when does further complexity remain relevant? It does so in three directions: (i) a three-phase historical mapping of the field's trajectory onto the bias--variance continuum, (ii) the introduction of \textit{informational efficiency} as a core evaluative concept, and (iii) a diagnosis of the ``illusion of optimality''---the risk that increasing complexity is mistaken for progress in the absence of shared efficiency metrics.

We specifically refer throughout to ``multi-risk'' frameworks as defined by \citet{zschau20172}: integrated systems that account for complex interactions among multiple hazards, exposed assets, and societal vulnerabilities---as distinct from simpler multi-hazard approaches that consider co-occurrence without systemic interdependencies. It is important to specify that regardless of the terminological scope---whether strictly multi-hazard (physical processes), multi-hazard risk (asset exposure), or multi-risk (systemic societal vulnerability)---the fundamental structural challenge remains analogous with the understanding that the rate of complexity increases at each step. Indeed each step up this hierarchy introduces new parameters, thereby increasing the risk of variance (overfitting) if the underlying process understanding does not scale at the same rate as the model's complexity.

As \citet{Dunant2021} highlighted, single-hazard models in multi-hazard environments exhibit high bias, while overly complex multi-risk models might show high variance without proportional gains in predictive accuracy (Figure~\ref{fig:BV-figure}). However, this framework has remained largely theoretical. The field lacks operationalisable metrics---quantitative where measurable, qualitative where not---to situate itself within this landscape. The central premise of this paper follows directly: \textit{we do not know where the field currently sits on the bias--variance curve}. It may be close to an optimal balance, still on the high-bias side, or already drifting toward inefficiency; none of these positions can presently be confirmed or ruled out.

This paper argues that \textit{informational efficiency} should become a core evaluative dimension of multi-risk science. We define it as the \textit{marginal} gain in uncertainty-aware, decision-relevant output obtained for each added increment of model complexity---for example, one additional hazard layer, interaction, or stakeholder input---relative to the simpler model without it. Framing efficiency at the margin is deliberate. The relevant question is not whether a complex model is useful overall, but whether each addition earns its place, which is the logic of value-of-information analysis \citep{howard1966information, keisler2014value} and of the distinction between forecast quality and forecast value \citep{murphy1993good}. The concept further draws on decision theory \citep{Horvitz1995DisplayOIA}, information theory \citep{shannon1948mathematical, cover2006elements}, and risk communication research \citep{Klinke2002ANAB}. In the context herein, informational efficiency is distinct from traditional statistical accuracy. In multi-risk environments, a purely quantitative ``performance'' metric risks missing the local social features and contexts that are fundamental to risk decision-making. The marginal gain can therefore be expressed in several currencies, quantitative and qualitative---statistical, decisional, temporal, institutional, interpretive, and social---rather than a single rigid metric; candidate markers are proposed in Table~\ref{tab:efficiency_metrics}. We acknowledge that explicitly measuring efficiency may not in itself dissolve the illusion of optimality, particularly when social systems resist the very notion of a clear-cut and universal optimum. The argument is therefore not that better metrics will solve the problem, but that without any shared language of efficiency, the field cannot even diagnose where it stands.

\section{Conceptual Framework}
\label{sec:framework}
The bias--variance framework is used here, as in \citet{Dunant2021}, as a conceptual heuristic---not as a literal statistical mapping. In statistical learning theory, bias and variance are mathematically decomposable components of prediction error; in multi-risk science, they are not. The analogy is therefore diagnostic rather than quantitative: it provides a vocabulary for asking whether a given integration effort evolves toward oversimplification (high bias) or toward unmanageable complexity (high variance).

The concern is not new to environmental modelling. The difficulty of validating open-system models against an unambiguous ground truth \citep{oreskes1994verification}, the equifinality of competing model structures \citep{beven2006manifesto}, and the risk of getting ``the right answers for the wrong reasons'' \citep{kirchner2006getting} have long motivated calls for parsimony and iterative model evaluation \citep{box1976science, jakeman2006ten}. More recently, \citet{puy2022models} showed across a large sample of environmental and epidemiological models that models with higher effective dimensions tend to produce more uncertain estimates, and \citet{saltelli2020five} warned that complexity pursued for its own sake can undermine the relevance of models to the societies they serve. The opposite position has also been argued: over-simplified models can hide structural error and underestimate predictive uncertainty \citep{hunt2007models}. In machine learning, the classical U-shaped risk curve has itself been qualified by the ``double descent'' behaviour of heavily over-parameterised models \citep{belkin2019reconciling}. We note this caveat explicitly, because it cautions against assuming that more complexity \textit{must} mean more variance. In multi-risk assessment, however, the data regimes that make double descent possible are rarely available, and the relevant costs of complexity are as much cognitive and institutional as they are statistical. Behavioural research makes a parallel point: simple heuristics can outperform complex strategies precisely because they trade a little bias for a large reduction in variance \citep{gigerenzer2009homo, gigerenzer2011heuristic}.

This lens applies beyond physical models. In participatory exercises, the bias--variance logic concerns not the value of any particular knowledge, but how many inputs are combined and how. Heterogeneous and sometimes incommensurable knowledge claims from diverse disciplinary and stakeholder perspectives are a valuable reflection of complex social realities \citep{Schlter2024NavigatingCRA, rusca_water_2026}, and including them can reduce bias by bringing in knowledge that technical models miss. Yet, as with added model parameters, each additional input also carries a cost. When contributions accumulate faster than the means to reconcile them, the result can be redundancy, unresolved conflict between inputs, and difficulty reaching an actionable synthesis---a participatory analogue of variance, in which informational efficiency falls without a commensurate gain in decision relevance. Interpretability suffers too: if practitioners cannot trace the causal logic across competing narratives, they cannot assume the professional responsibility required to translate assessments into action \citep{pereira2024integrating}. The difficulty, in other words, lies not in participation itself but in integration without a way of judging what each contribution adds (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

In this context, the pursuit of ``multi-risk'' optimality must account for bounded rationality \citep{Simon1956, Simon1947} and acknowledge the absence of ``unlimited resources of time, information, and computational might'' \citep{gigerenzer2020bounded}. Analogous to the ``efficiency--resilience'' tradeoff \citep{Hynes2020}, hyper-integration (low bias) could produce an ``integration--resilience tradeoff'', where noise overtakes the capacity for action.

The following sections categorise the evolution of multi-risk science into three chronological phases and read them against the bias--variance continuum \citep{Dunant2021} (Figure~\ref{fig:BV-figure}). An important caveat applies. The phases describe the \textit{direction} in which the field has moved---toward more integration and more parameters---not its \textit{position} on the curve. For this reason, Figure~\ref{fig:BV-figure} deliberately places the phases beside the error curve rather than on it: without measures of informational efficiency, we cannot say whether any phase lies to the left of the optimum, at it, or beyond it.

It does not locate the field on the curve; instead, it frames the question of whether current integration efforts achieve functional efficiency or merely drift toward an ``illusion of optimality''.

\section{Evolution of multi-risk science}

\subsection{Phase I: Siloed Origins and Emerging Integration Mandates (before 2005)}
The first attempts to formalise multi-hazard assessment emerged alongside early international frameworks promoting integrated disaster management. The idea itself is older: \citet{hewitt1971hazardousness} had already argued for studying ``all hazards at a place'' rather than one hazard at a time. On the science side, the volcanology community provided significant early precedents, as volcanic crises inherently involve the interaction of multiple simultaneous hazards like ashfall, lahars, and seismic activity \citep{tilling1989volcanic, blong1996volcanic}; event trees were developed to structure these interacting outcomes during crises \citep{newhall2002constructing}. These were, however, exceptions driven by the physics of a single hazard source rather than a general practice across the field.

The principles of sustainable development outlined in \textit{Agenda 21} \citep{Agenda21_1992} and later reinforced by the Johannesburg Plan \citep{UN2002} and the Hyogo Framework for Action \citep{HyogoFramework2005} established the groundwork for multi-hazard thinking. Figure~\ref{fig:history} shows the institutional momentum that followed: annual publication counts from three independent bibliometric reviews \citep{Tilloy2019, Owolabi2023, grandjean2025bibliographic} rise slowly before the Hyogo Framework and accelerate after it, and again after Sendai. These counts track research activity, not model complexity or performance; they show how quickly the field grew, not whether that growth moved it toward the optimum.

During this phase, risk assessments remained largely disciplinary, emphasising individual hazards such as floods, earthquakes, or landslides. Studies like \citet{Middelmann2000} and \citet{granger1999community} in Australia exemplified early local-scale multi-hazard assessments, but these typically overlaid independent single-hazard analyses rather than modelling interactions between them \citep{kappes_challenges_2012, gill2014reviewing}. In bias--variance terms, most models of this period sat on the high-bias side: simplified assumptions and a focus on single-event probabilities, regardless of whether events occurred in a multi-hazard environment.

Uncertainty was rarely quantified explicitly, and hazards were often combined through qualitative or semi-quantitative matrices \citep{kappes_challenges_2012, gallina2016review}. The resulting models were interpretable but detached from cascading dynamics and long-term systemic risks.

\subsection{Phase II: Institutional Integration and the Illusion of Optimality (2005–?)}

Following the Hyogo Framework, institutional momentum accelerated the integration of multiple hazards. The Sendai Framework \citep{SendaiFramework2015} later reaffirmed this mandate, emphasizing "all-of-society" and "systemic" approaches. These international mandates, by prioritizing 'holistic' and 'all-hazard' frameworks, effectively incentivized the creation of comprehensive systems designed to map every interaction. Conceptual work by \citet{Marzocchi2009, marzocchi2012basic} and \citet{kappes_challenges_2012} articulated the need for frameworks addressing multi-hazard and multi-risk interactions, since single-hazard approaches in multi-hazard environments are inherently biased toward specific features and miss critical interdependencies. Systematic classifications of hazard interactions \citep{gill2014reviewing, gill2016hazard} and reviews of multi-risk methodologies \citep{gallina2016review} gave this agenda a common vocabulary. The transition from single-hazard to systemic multi-risk assessments is, in part, driven by what \citet{zschau20172} and \citet{hochrainer2024managing} identify as the twin challenges of risk globalisation and climate change. Globalisation has rendered risks interdependent through cross-linked critical infrastructure and global supply chains, while climate change alters the frequency, intensity and spatial distribution of extreme events \citep{seneviratne2021weather}, including compound events whose joint occurrence drives impacts \citep{zscheischler2018future, zscheischler2020typology} and consecutive disasters that strike before recovery is complete \citep{deruiter2020why}. These drivers create ``high-risk dynamics'' that require capturing various interactions \citep{pescaroli2018understanding}, and in doing so intensify the bias--variance dilemma highlighted herein.

Research in the modelling space during this phase sought to reduce bias by linking hazard chains, formalising interactions statistically \citep{selva2013long, mignan2014quantification}, introducing Bayesian networks, and incorporating network-based or spatio-temporal frameworks \citep{Tilloy2019, DeAngeli2022, Terzi2019, dunant2021multihazards, dunant2025impacts}. These efforts represented a major step toward a unified understanding of cascading effects and feedback loops. In bias--variance terms, they were designed to reduce bias; whether they also increased variance, and by how much, was not measured.

Yet the apparent convergence toward optimality might be deceptive. Several reviews show that integration often increased conceptual overlap but still struggled to translate complexity into actionable indicators \citep{Owolabi2023, laino2024scientometric, hochrainer2024managing, scolobig2017mainstreaming}. Feedback from civil protection stakeholders on multi-risk decision-support tools pointed to the same gap between what the tools offered and what practitioners could use \citep{komendantova2014multi}, a pattern also found in broader reviews of decision support systems for natural hazards \citep{newman2017review}. The field seems to achieve visibility but not necessarily efficiency---that is, the marginal gain in decision relevance or process understanding obtained for each added increment of complexity. The illusion of optimality persisted not simply because standardised metrics were absent, but because the very notion of what constitutes an informational gain was never made explicit: whether efficiency should be measured in terms of improved risk communication, facilitated action, or deeper process understanding remained unarticulated. This ambiguity---not merely a technical gap---is what could allow increasing complexity to be mistaken for progress. \citet{Dunant2021}'s warning that as model complexity increases, the risk of ``overfitting'' escalates, potentially impairing the practical precision of multi-hazard outputs, is even more pertinent today as the tools for large-scale computation and collaboration become more accessible \citep{mack2011fifty}.

\subsection{Phase III: Rising Complexity and the Unquantified Present (?)}
While the recent proliferation of highly complex, socially integrated models might suggest the field has already transitioned into a "Phase III" of multi-risk assessment, we argue that this boundary remains entirely unverified. Because the field lacks operationalised metrics for informational efficiency, we simply cannot know whether the current landscape represents a genuine paradigm shift toward optimal risk integration, or merely an inflation of variance and epistemic noise. Consequently, Phase III remains an unquantified horizon rather than a confirmed reality. Nevertheless, we argue that the field (i.e.\ multi-hazard, multi-risk) stands at a crossroads today. Advances in computational modelling, remote sensing, and machine learning have enabled ``hyper-integrated'' frameworks spanning climate, geophysical, and social domains \citep{Ferrario2025, MYRIAD2022, lundstrom2022moore}. Conceptual frameworks for complex and systemic risk now explicitly call for interactions between hazards, between responses, and across sectors and scales to be represented \citep{simpson2021framework, renn2022systemic, hochrainer2023toward, ward2022invited}, while vulnerability itself is increasingly treated as dynamic rather than fixed \citep{deruiter2022challenges}. However, these systems risk overfitting: absorbing massive input data into interconnected systems without tangible, measurable improvement in output quality or decision usability.

As demonstrated by recent assessments of European transboundary risk management, the primary hurdle to operationalising multi-risk science is not a lack of model complexity, but a lack of fundamental harmonisation in metrics and data structures across borders \citep{polese2024multi}. Workshops with European stakeholders likewise identified governance, the translation of science to policy and practice, and data availability---rather than modelling capacity---among the main barriers to multi-hazard risk management \citep{sakictrogrlic2024challenges}. While scientific ``super-models'' attempt to simulate complex hazard interactions, practitioners are often still restricted by a ``fragmented data landscape'' and a lack of common vulnerability classes, which makes the outputs of highly integrated models difficult to compare or legally validate in an operational context \citep{polese2024multi}. Furthermore, as \citet{kalogiannidis2024analyzing} highlight, the effectiveness of disaster management in Europe is more significantly hindered by administrative and legislative obstacles—such as the lack of standardised protocols—than by a lack of computational integration. This suggests that the ``illusion of optimality'' in complex modelling fails to account for the ``social reality'' of the legal and administrative constraints within which Civil Protection agencies must operate.

Informational efficiency, in the marginal sense defined above, remains largely untracked: few studies report what an added hazard layer, interaction, or stakeholder input contributes relative to the simpler model without it. Without such evidence, integration can drift into epistemic opacity, where uncertainty and redundancy coexist. As \citet{dunant2021multihazards} emphasised, finding a ``healthy medium between accuracy (bias) and precision (variance)'' requires quantifying the relationship between model complexity, risk and action.

Future progress depends on being honest about what kind of progress is being made. The objectives of \textit{scientific progress} (advancing process understanding) and \textit{operational progress} (supporting defensible decisions) are not opposed, but they optimise differently and require different standards of evaluation. Conflating them risks producing the ``Mushy Middle'' \citep{stonehouse2007competitive}: work that achieves neither genuine process insight nor novel operational application---sophisticated enough to appear rigorous, tractable enough to appear useful, but evaluated against neither standard. For the researcher, the relevant question is therefore not whether a model is complex, but whether its complexity resolves a specific gap in process understanding that simpler models cannot. For the practitioner, the question is whether it narrows the range of defensible decisions under uncertainty in a way that is institutionally actionable \citep{polese2024multi}. In both cases, the threshold for ``good enough'' is as much social and institutional as it is technical---it depends on who will use the output, under what constraints, and toward what decision \citep{HangerKopp2022, Simon1956}. Research on usable science reaches the same conclusion: information is taken up when it is credible, salient, and legitimate to its users \citep{cash2003knowledge}, and when producers and users interact to close the ``usability gap'' \citep{dilling2011creating, lemos2012narrowing, kirchhoff2013actionable}. Where the supply of scientific information is not matched to the demands of decision-makers, more research does not translate into better decisions \citep{sarewitz2007neglected, weichselgartner2010barriers}.

What the field currently lacks might not be more integration, but an explicit, shared account of these thresholds. Without it, efficiency claims remain unverifiable, complexity continues to be equated with progress, and the illusion of optimality persists \citep{scolobig2017mainstreaming, kalogiannidis2024analyzing}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The three phases outlined above trace a field moving from high-bias simplicity toward greater complexity, without a clear mechanism for evaluating its actual position on the curve or whether this trajectory constitutes progress. Several observations emerge.

First, the bias--variance analogy illuminates but does not resolve. In statistical learning, model selection can be guided by cross-validation against held-out data; in multi-risk science, an equivalent ground truth against which to calibrate is rarely available, because the systems being modelled are open and the events of interest are rare \citep{oreskes1994verification}. The analogy therefore functions as a diagnostic vocabulary---useful for framing the problem, but insufficient as a prescriptive tool.

Second, the distinction between scientific and operational progress---raised in Phase~III---deserves further attention. The ``Mushy Middle'' \citep{stonehouse2007competitive}, originally a concept from competitive strategy describing firms that pursue neither cost leadership nor differentiation, finds a parallel in multi-risk initiatives that achieve neither deep process understanding nor operational tractability. Recognising this risk does not require abandoning integration, but it does require that researchers and practitioners articulate---before model development---which standard of evaluation applies.

Third, while informational efficiency is defined here as a marginal quantity, its \textit{measurement} is deliberately left open. Premature formalisation risks privileging one definition of ``useful output'' over others. Nevertheless, the field would benefit from pilot studies that attempt to quantify efficiency in bounded contexts---for example, by comparing the marginal decision value of adding a second hazard layer to a single-hazard assessment in a specific governance setting. Well-established tools already exist for parts of this task, from value-of-information analysis \citep{howard1966information, keisler2014value} to the economic value of forecasts under cost--loss decisions \citep{richardson2000skill, pappenberger2015monetary}. These tools apply where the gain can be measured.

Fourth, much of what can make added complexity worthwhile cannot be reduced to a number. Once social components become central, the gain from an added layer may lie in a shared understanding of the problem, in the inclusion of previously ignored knowledge, or in the legitimacy of a decision---none of which has a natural metric. Multi-risk problems share many features of what \citet{rittel1973dilemmas} called ``wicked'' problems and of post-normal science, in which facts are uncertain, values in dispute, stakes high, and decisions urgent \citep{funtowicz1993science}. In such settings, the quality of an assessment cannot be judged by predictive skill alone, and uncertainty includes ignorance and indeterminacy as well as statistical variability \citep{wynne1992uncertainty, walker2003defining}. Relying on quantitative markers alone would itself introduce a bias: toward the parts of an assessment that happen to be easy to measure.

The marginal logic of informational efficiency still applies; only the evidence changes. The question remains whether an added component earns its place relative to the assessment without it. Established approaches already give structure to such judgements. Pedigree analysis within the NUSAP system scores the strength of the knowledge behind each model input---its empirical basis, methodological rigour, and degree of validation---so that an added hazard layer can be judged against the quality of what it rests on \citep{funtowicz1990uncertainty, vandersluijs2005combining}. Sensitivity auditing extends this scrutiny to the framing and assumptions of a model \citep{saltelli2013what}. Adequacy-for-purpose evaluation asks not whether a model is accurate overall but whether it is fit for a specific use \citep{parker2020model}. Storylines offer a qualitative alternative to adding probabilistic layers, representing plausible physical pathways of an event in a form decision-makers can reason with \citep{shepherd2018storylines, sillmann2021event}. The criteria of credibility, salience, and legitimacy \citep{cash2003knowledge}, together with principles for knowledge co-production and participatory modelling \citep{hegger2012conceptualising, voinov2016modelling, norstrom2020principles}, provide a basis for judging whether added participatory complexity improves the knowledge produced rather than merely its volume. Evidence for these judgements can be gathered through structured interviews, mental-model elicitation \citep{morgan2002risk}, workshop observation, and post-event reviews. Where uncertainty is deep, the aim shifts from identifying an optimal model to identifying decisions that remain robust across plausible futures \citep{stirling2010keep, marchau2019decision}.

\section{Conclusion}

The history of multi-risk assessment shows a clear direction of travel---from siloed, high-bias assessments toward increasingly integrated and complex ones---but not a known position. Whether the field today sits short of the bias--variance optimum, near it, or beyond it cannot be established with the evidence currently collected. This perspective suggests that increasing model complexity does not necessarily improve understanding or decision relevance, as integration can introduce additional uncertainty and interpretability challenges \citep{puy2022models, saltelli2020five}. Recent advances in holistic frameworks and AI-driven systems highlight both the potential and the difficulty of managing these trade-offs \citep{ward2026reducing, reichstein2025early}.

Building on the bias–variance perspective introduced by \citet{Dunant2021}, this paper argues that the central challenge is not only methodological but epistemic. Greater integration must be accompanied by clearer research intent and a systematic evaluation of informational efficiency---namely, the marginal gain in uncertainty-aware, usable output that each added increment of complexity delivers. The bias–variance framework therefore serves not as an end, but as a diagnostic tool to assess whether added complexity yields meaningful gains.

To address this, we propose a set of quantitative and qualitative informational-efficiency markers (Table~\ref{tab:efficiency_metrics}). Each compares an assessment with a simpler version of itself, and together they span statistical, decisional, temporal, institutional, interpretive, and social gains. The qualitative markers matter as much as the quantitative ones: where the value of an assessment lies in shared understanding or legitimacy, judging it only by what can be measured would reintroduce the very bias this framework is meant to expose. The markers do not define a universal optimum, but they give the field a shared way of asking whether an added layer of complexity has earned its place.

Establishing such approaches is essential to ensure that the evolution of multi-risk modelling remains both rigorous and actionable in the context of increasingly interconnected global risks.
