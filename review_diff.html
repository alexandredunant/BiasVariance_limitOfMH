A Discussion section synthesises across the three phases, acknowledges the analogy's limitations, and sharpens the forward-looking argument. Insert before the Conclusion.
\section{Discussion} The three phases outlined above trace a field that has moved from high-bias simplicity to high-variance complexity without a clear mechanism for evaluating whether this trajectory constitutes progress. Several observations emerge from this mapping. First, the bias--variance analogy illuminates but does not resolve. In statistical learning, model selection can be guided by cross-validation against held-out data; in multi-risk science, no equivalent ground truth exists against which to calibrate. The analogy therefore functions as a diagnostic vocabulary---useful for framing the problem, but insufficient as a prescriptive tool. Future work should explore whether proxy metrics drawn from information theory (e.g., mutual information between model inputs and decision outputs) or decision analysis (e.g., value of information) can partially operationalise this vocabulary. Second, the distinction between scientific and operational progress---raised in Phase~III---deserves further attention. The ``Mushy Middle'' \citep{stonehouse2007competitive}, originally a concept from competitive strategy describing firms that pursue neither cost leadership nor differentiation, finds a parallel in multi-risk models that achieve neither deep process understanding nor operational tractability. Recognising this risk does not require abandoning integration, but it does require that researchers and practitioners articulate---before model development---which standard of evaluation applies. Third, informational efficiency as proposed here remains deliberately underspecified in its measurement. This is intentional: premature formalisation risks privileging one definition of ``useful output'' over others. Nevertheless, the field would benefit from pilot studies that attempt to quantify efficiency in bounded contexts---for example, by comparing the marginal decision value of adding a second hazard layer to a single-hazard assessment in a specific governance setting.
